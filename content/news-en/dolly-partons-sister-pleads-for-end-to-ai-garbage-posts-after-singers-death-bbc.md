Following the death of country music icon Dolly Parton on August 25 at the age of 80, her sister Stella Parton has publicly criticized the influx of synthetic media online, labeling the trend "fake AI garbage."

Writing on Instagram, Stella Parton expressed gratitude to fans for genuine tributes while calling out the overwhelming volume of automated posts and deepfakes that have emerged across major platforms. She noted that public figures and their grieving families are subjected to intense insensitivity when personal loss is exploited for online engagement.

## Proliferation of Synthetic Tributes

Since Parton's passing, digital platforms have seen a surge in AI-generated material ranging from manipulated photos to synthesized songs mimicking well-known artists. 

On YouTube, a computer-generated audio track impersonating Miley Cyrus paying tribute to her godmother gathered over 1.5 million views. Although the uploader included a disclaimer stating the content was AI-generated and unofficial, thousands of commenters responded as if the recording were authentic.

Synthetic imagery has also circulated widely across platforms such as X, Instagram, and Truth Social—including an AI-rendered photo posted by Donald Trump depicting himself alongside the late singer. Other generated images falsely depicting Parton without her signature makeup or wigs drew sharp criticism from fans, who labeled the content intrusive.

## A Pattern of Grief Exploitation

Stella Parton’s public appeal mirrors warnings previously raised by relatives of other high-profile figures. In recent months, Zelda Williams, daughter of the late Robin Williams, and Amy Redford, daughter of Robert Redford, both issued public pleas urging internet users to stop generating and sharing synthetic deepfakes of their deceased family members.

Ironically, Dolly Parton herself expressed unease regarding the rapid rise of generative technology during a 2023 interview with *The Hollywood Reporter*. While acknowledging potential benefits in fields like medicine and science, she voiced strong opposition to using technology to replace human identity and artistry.

As generative tools become increasingly accessible to the public, the reaction surrounding Parton's death underscores a growing tension between viral engagement tactics and the ethical boundaries of digital legacy.
