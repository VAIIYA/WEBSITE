Political resistance is growing in the United States against the use of automatic license plate reader (ALPR) cameras on public roads. Both Democratic and Republican lawmakers are pushing for strict legal rules for these smart devices. Privacy concerns are mounting quickly as the integration of artificial intelligence dramatically expands the surveillance system's capabilities.

Tech company Flock Safety in particular is under scrutiny. The company's cameras continuously collect and analyze vehicle data on public roads. Supporters see the technology as an efficient way to safeguard public safety, but critics warn of an uncontrolled expansion of the surveillance state.

A recent incident in Florida underscores that fear. A police officer in Sarasota was fired and arrested after misusing the Flock system more than 300 times to spy on one specific license plate.

## Political divide gives way to shared privacy concerns

Republican Representative Greg Steube calls monitoring citizens without clear legal frameworks a violation of the Fourth Amendment of the US Constitution. His fellow party member Keith Self also warns that the newest generation of AI-powered cameras goes far beyond simply reading license plates. According to him, the technology now makes it possible to observe vehicle occupants.

A similar sentiment is heard on the Democratic side. Representative Delia Ramirez argues that commercial tech companies are making huge profits from mass surveillance, which she says poses a direct threat to democracy and civil rights. Ahead of the midterm elections, the issue has become a major campaign topic in several states, including Michigan.

Several members of Congress, including Tim Burchett and Thomas Massie, are now working on federal legislation to rein in the use of the equipment. Various municipalities, such as Lansing, Michigan, and Bernalillo County, New Mexico, have already ended or reconsidered their partnerships with Flock Safety.

## Defense from the industry and law enforcement

Garrett Langley, founder and CEO of Flock Safety, defends the use of the cameras. He points out that the technology has demonstrably helped police find missing children and solve violent crimes. Langley emphasizes that his company does not use facial recognition and is not calling for a ban, but rather for clear legislation and accountability, comparable to traffic rules for seatbelts.

Nuance is also being called for within law enforcement and think tanks. Rafael Mangual of the Manhattan Institute calls the cameras a net positive tool. According to him, the successful investigations far outweigh the occasional cases of abuse. Jim Pasco of the National Fraternal Order of Police likewise acknowledges the technology's value, but stresses that clear regulation is needed to prevent abuse without completely blocking its useful applications.
