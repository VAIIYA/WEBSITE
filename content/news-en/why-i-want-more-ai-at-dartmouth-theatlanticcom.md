As generative artificial intelligence transforms classrooms and entry-level job markets, universities face a critical strategic choice: embrace the technology or attempt to restrict its access. While several prominent institutions have opted for strict prohibitions, Dartmouth College President Sian Leah Beilock argues that banning AI risks making higher education irrelevant in an increasingly automated world.

Writing in *The Atlantic*, Beilock—a cognitive scientist specializing in performance anxiety—compares the current academic unease around AI to math anxiety. Her research indicates that fear of technology, much like fear of mathematics, is best mitigated through direct practice and structured exposure rather than avoidance.

## The Case Against Campus Bans

The urge to restrict AI stems largely from concerns over academic integrity and real labor market disruption. Recent research from Stanford University reveals a nearly 20 percent decline in employment for young workers in AI-exposed roles, such as software engineering and customer service.

In response, several top universities have implemented strict bans. The UC Berkeley School of Law has barred students from using AI tools for drafting, conceptualizing, or editing coursework, while the University of Chicago Law School prohibited all screens from first-year classrooms.

Beilock cautions that such prohibitionist approaches abdicate higher education's core duty to prepare students for modern careers. If universities refuse to guide ethical AI deployment, she warns, the private sector will set those boundaries without academic oversight or ethical framing.

## Practical Integration at Dartmouth

Instead of restricting access, Dartmouth is integrating AI literacy directly into its academic curriculum. The university has introduced specialized positions, such as critical AI librarians, to help students identify algorithmic biases and verify claims generated by large language models.

At Dartmouth's DALI Lab, undergraduates build and stress-test custom AI models to better grasp technical limitations. Specialized programs are also underway across Dartmouth's professional schools:

* **Tuck School of Business:** Requires first-year students to build AI-driven client solutions within core project work.
* **Geisel School of Medicine:** Developed an "AI Patient Actor" to help medical trainees hone interpersonal communication skills—a tool now adopted by over 100 medical institutions globally.

## Preparing Graduates for a Shifting Market

Data from PwC shows that demand for advanced AI skills is expanding eight times faster than the broader job market. Furthermore, entry-level positions in fields heavily affected by automation are seven times more likely to require high-level human capabilities, such as leadership, discernment, and ethical judgment.

Beilock asserts that higher education must focus on cultivating these distinctly human traits alongside technical fluency. Rather than shielding students from disruptive software, universities must equip them to evaluate, critique, and direct artificial intelligence effectively.
