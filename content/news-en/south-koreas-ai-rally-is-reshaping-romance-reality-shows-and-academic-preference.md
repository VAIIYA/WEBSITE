The global surge in artificial intelligence hardware demand is doing more than just filling corporate balance sheets—it is actively transforming social norms, media, and academic choices in South Korea.

Surging share prices for domestic tech titans are redefining what makes a job attractive, prompting shifts in everything from university major selections to popular dating shows.

## Shifting Preferences in the Dating Scene

In South Korean culture, professions such as doctors, lawyers, and judges have traditionally topped matchmaking wish lists. However, lucrative compensation packages in the semiconductor sector are disrupting those standards. 

Both Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix entered the trillion-dollar club earlier this year, supported by share price increases of roughly 95% and 135%, respectively. These market gains translate directly into massive financial windfalls for workers. At SK Hynix, profit-sharing agreements distributed roughly 700 million won (over $500,000) per employee—more than 15 times the national average annual salary.

Matchmaking agency Gayeon reported a noticeable increase in clients requesting partners working at chipmakers and AI firms. Korean singles are increasingly viewing marriage as a financial mechanism—sometimes referred to as "wedding leverage"—to combine incomes for home deposits and investment portfolios.

## Media Captures the Financial Craze

The rising profile of tech workers has quickly caught the attention of media producers. YouTube production team Teo launched a reality dating show specifically featuring single employees from Samsung and SK Hynix. The show quickly amassed over 650,000 views by blending workplace realities with romance.

Streaming platforms are also capitalizing on retail trading enthusiasm. Netflix released "Rookie Kim's Stock Market Mission," a variety show following a popular comedian purchasing shares in SK Hynix. The concept strongly appeals to South Korea's massive retail trading demographic, which encompasses roughly one in four citizens.

## A Rush Toward Tech Degrees

The immediate economic rewards of the chip industry are altering higher education paths at top-tier institutions. At Seoul National University, electrical and computer engineering has seen an unprecedented jump in demand.

Data shows that 90% of undeclared engineering majors chose electrical and computer engineering this year, up sharply from just 20% in 2023. Education experts note that students are deliberately targeting a direct, reliable career pipeline into high-paying hardware roles at the nation's dominant semiconductor manufacturers.
