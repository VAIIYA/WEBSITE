## The problem both companies are trying to solve

AI companies need some way to catch people misusing their tools — for example, someone slowly trying to get an AI's help building malware, spread out carefully across many separate conversations so no single message looks suspicious on its own. But checking for that kind of pattern usually means keeping some record of what customers are asking, which understandably makes privacy-conscious businesses nervous.

## OpenAI's approach: check everything, keep nothing

OpenAI's new system, called Private Safety Processing, uses automated review across many customer conversations to spot patterns that look like coordinated misuse — without permanently storing the actual customer data itself. If something suspicious turns up, the system sends OpenAI only a narrow signal flagging the issue, and a human at OpenAI then decides whether to actually take action. Customers can still choose to voluntarily share more detail if they want to help investigate something specific.

## How this differs from Anthropic's approach

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude family of AI models, announced back in July that it would start keeping certain customer data for 30 days for some of its models, specifically so it could look back and investigate potential misuse. That policy reportedly worried some enterprise customers who handle sensitive information and don't want their data sitting around anywhere longer than necessary, even for safety reasons.

## Why this rivalry matters right now

Both companies are locked in an increasingly intense competition for large business customers, and both are reportedly preparing to eventually go public. Anthropic's revenue has apparently been growing very quickly, while OpenAI's growth has been comparatively slower in recent months — so a feature like this, aimed squarely at businesses worried about data privacy, is as much a competitive jab at Anthropic as it is a genuine safety improvement.
